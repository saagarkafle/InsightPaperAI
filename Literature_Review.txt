Literature Review: Automated Research Paper Summarisation, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and Question Answering

1. Introduction
The volume of scientific literature published globally is expanding at an exponential rate. Researchers, academics, and students across virtually all technical disciplines face an overwhelming corpus of academic papers, preprints, and conference proceedings that cannot feasibly be read in full. Traditional manual reading methods struggle to keep pace with this influx, creating significant cognitive overload, information processing bottlenecks, and lost productivity during literature reviews.

To address this challenge, contemporary Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques provide automated methods for parsing, indexing, summarising, and extracting knowledge from academic literature. Recent advancements in deep learning, specifically transformer-based architectures, dense vector representation learning, and Large Language Models (LLMs), have transformed document processing and information retrieval. This literature review examines the theoretical foundations, algorithmic components, fine-tuning methodologies, and evaluation frameworks necessary to build an end-to-end system capable of parsing scientific PDFs, performing dense semantic search, and answering natural language questions grounded strictly in paper context.

In the following sections, the core theoretical foundations of transformer-based embeddings, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), fine-tuning via contrastive learning, vector space index management, and quantitative evaluation metrics are critically reviewed to establish the scientific foundation for this research project.

2. Deep Learning and Document Processing Foundations
2.1. Transformer Architectures in Academic NLP
Modern natural language processing relies predominantly on the Transformer architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017). By replacing traditional recurrent neural networks (RNNs) with multi-head self-attention mechanisms, transformers capture long-range contextual dependencies across sequence tokens in parallel. This architectural leap enabled pre-trained language models to achieve state-of-the-art performance across document parsing, translation, summarisation, and reading comprehension.

In scientific literature processing, applying transformers serves two main goals: (1) mapping dense academic prose into continuous vector representations that capture deep semantic relationships, and (2) generating fluent, contextually accurate answers to user queries grounded strictly within retrieved paper context.

2.2. PDF Parsing, Document Structuring, and Text Chunking
Scientific publications present unique structural challenges compared to raw unstructured text. Research papers contain complex hierarchical elements including multi-column layouts, figures, mathematical equations, captions, and reference sections. Effective document ingestion requires robust extraction tools (such as PyMuPDF/fitz) to separate text streams, extract embedded images, and associate figures with corresponding caption text.

Furthermore, Large Language Models and embedding models operate under strict maximum token context windows. A complete research paper typically contains 5,000 to 20,000 words, far exceeding standard embedding model context limits (e.g., 512 tokens for MiniLM). Direct truncation risks dropping critical methodology or conclusion sections. To resolve this, text chunking protocols divide documents into smaller overlapping segments (e.g., 500 words per chunk with a 100-word sliding window overlap). The sliding window overlap preserves semantic continuity across chunk boundaries, ensuring that key conceptual transitions and multi-sentence statements are not fractured during indexing.

3. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and Semantic Search
3.1. Dense Vector Embeddings and Sentence Transformers
Traditional keyword matching algorithms (such as TF-IDF and BM25) rely on literal token matching, failing to capture semantic synonyms, paraphrases, or high-level conceptual context. Dense passage retrieval addresses this limitation by projecting text chunks into continuous low-dimensional vector spaces using Siamese transformer networks.

The Sentence-BERT framework and lightweight MiniLM variants like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) map sentences into dense 384-dimensional vector spaces. Within this manifold, geometric distances (such as cosine similarity) measure true semantic relevance regardless of surface-level vocabulary differences. For example, a query asking "What methodology was employed?" will map close to chunks describing "experimental setup", "model design", or "algorithmic implementation".

3.2. Embedding Model Fine-Tuning via Contrastive Learning
While general-purpose sentence transformers perform reasonably well on open-domain tasks, domain adaptation and task-specific fine-tuning significantly enhance retrieval precision for research Question Answering.

Fine-tuning embedding models on Reading Comprehension benchmarks such as SQuAD 2.0 (Stanford Question Answering Dataset; Rajpurkar et al., 2018) teaches the model to align natural language questions directly with passage contexts containing the answers. SQuAD 2.0 comprises over 150,000 question-context-answer triples derived from academic and reference articles.

To fine-tune dense sentence embedder models efficiently without explicit negative labeling, contrastive loss functions such as MultipleNegativesRankingLoss (MNRL) are employed. Given a batch of N (question, positive context) pairs, MNRL treats the positive context of the i-th question as the target, while using the remaining (N - 1) contexts in the mini-batch as implicit negative examples. The model minimizes the cross-entropy loss over softmax-normalized cosine similarity scores:

    Loss = -log( exp(sim(q_i, c_i) / τ) / Σ_j exp(sim(q_i, c_j) / τ) )

This contrastive objective pulls matching question-context pairs closer together in vector space while actively pushing non-matching pairs further apart. Empirically, fine-tuning lightweight models (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2) on SQuAD 2.0 increases retrieval margins (the gap between correct context similarity and distractor context similarity) by upwards of 50%, directly improving downstream RAG retrieval accuracy.

3.3. Cloud Vector Databases and High-Dimensional Indexing
Searching dense vector collections linearly across thousands of chunks exhibits O(N) time complexity, becoming a performance bottleneck as document libraries scale. Vector databases solve this by organizing high-dimensional embeddings into optimized index structures.

Cloud-native vector database platforms like Pinecone manage high-dimensional index management using serverless architectures. By storing 384-dimensional dense vectors alongside rich payload metadata (such as paper_id, chunk_index, source_type, and text snippets), vector databases enable instant cosine similarity retrieval with metadata filtering. This metadata-filtered retrieval allows users to scope semantic searches dynamically to specific research papers, user datasets, or global collections.

3.4. Grounding Answers and Hallucination Mitigation
General-purpose Large Language Models (LLMs) suffer from hallucination, generating plausible-sounding but factual ungrounded statements or invalid citations (Ji et al., 2023). Retrieval-Augmented Generation (P. Lewis et al., 2020; Karpukhin et al., 2020) resolves this by dynamically retrieving the top-K relevant document chunks via vector similarity search before passing them to an instruction-tuned LLM (such as LLaMA 3.1 via high-throughput inference backends like Groq).

By enforcing strict system prompt constraints that compel the LLM to generate answers strictly from the provided context chunks, RAG eliminates hallucination, provides transparent source attribution, and guarantees verifiable answers grounded directly in the target research paper.

4. Evaluation Frameworks for RAG and Question Answering Systems
4.1. Automated Question Answering Evaluation Metrics
Evaluating the quality of generated answers in RAG pipelines against gold-standard reference answers requires quantitative, multi-perspective evaluation metrics:
* Exact Match (EM): The percentage of model predictions that match the ground-truth answer string character-for-character after standard text normalization (Rajpurkar et al., 2018).
* Token-Level F1 Score: The harmonic mean of token-level precision and recall between predicted and reference answers. It rewards partial matches and accommodates minor lexical variations:
      Precision = |Predicted ∩ Reference| / |Predicted|
      Recall = |Predicted ∩ Reference| / |Reference|
      F1 = 2 * (Precision * Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
* Semantic Vector Similarity: Measures the cosine similarity between the 384-dimensional dense embeddings of the predicted answer and the reference answer:
      Semantic Similarity = (v_pred · v_ref) / (||v_pred|| * ||v_ref||)
  Unlike F1, semantic similarity captures conceptual alignment even when the model rephrases the answer using entirely different vocabulary (Zhang et al., 2020).

4.2. Retrieval Margin Analysis
Evaluating retrieval quality independently of LLM generation is critical for diagnosing RAG bottlenecks. The Retrieval Margin measures the similarity gap between a query's correct context passage (c_pos) and a top distractor context passage (c_neg):
      Margin = CosineSimilarity(query, c_pos) - CosineSimilarity(query, c_neg)

A higher positive margin indicates that the embedding model effectively segregates relevant information from noise, ensuring that the downstream LLM receives accurate context within its top-K retrieved window.

5. Conclusion and Core Research Questions
The literature confirms that integrating PDF parsing, fine-tuned dense vector embeddings (Sentence-Transformers), cloud vector databases (Pinecone), and instruction-tuned generative LLMs (LLaMA 3.1) under a Retrieval-Augmented Generation architecture offers a scalable solution for scientific literature analysis.

Based on the theoretical gaps and implementation requirements identified in the literature, this research project addresses the following primary research question:
* How effectively can an end-to-end RAG architecture utilizing a fine-tuned Sentence-Transformer embedder, Pinecone vector index, and LLaMA 3.1 LLM retrieve relevant paper contexts and generate accurate, source-grounded answers for academic research papers?

To answer this overarching question, the following sub-questions guide system design, empirical implementation, and quantitative evaluation:
1. To what extent does fine-tuning a lightweight embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) on SQuAD 2.0 via MultipleNegativesRankingLoss improve the retrieval margin between correct and distractor contexts?
2. How do token-level F1 scores and dense semantic similarity metrics compare when evaluating model-generated answers against ground-truth dataset answers?
3. How effectively can source-type metadata filtering (PDF vs. Dataset) isolate document domain contexts while maintaining real-time query latency (< 1.5s)?
